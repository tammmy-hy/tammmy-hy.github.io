Overview

This three-week exhibition design project explored how visitor movement, crowding, and engagement shape experiences at the Vancouver Aquarium.

Through observation, persona development, and concept iteration, our team designed an immersive Arctic Projection Tunnel that encourages exploration of underutilized exhibits while supporting learning through interaction.

Research

Initial Framing

How might we understand how different visitor groups navigate and experience the Vancouver Aquarium?

Reframed Design Opportunity

How might we support different visitor groups in engaging with the Vancouver Aquarium during crowded moments while balancing active exploration and informational learning?

Key Research Findings

Insight 01

Visitors naturally gravitate toward scheduled activities, creating predictable crowding patterns.

Insight 02

Perceived value influences movement more than physical layout.

Insight 03

Different visitor groups require different forms of engagement.

Insight 04

Crowded environments shift attention from learning to securing sightlines.

Feedback

During concept development, our professor encouraged us to move beyond solving crowding directly and instead focus on redistributing visitor attention toward underutilized areas of the aquarium. They also challenged us to reconsider our personas, grounding them more closely in observational evidence rather than broad stereotypes. These critiques shifted the project from a crowd-management problem toward an experience design opportunity centered on engagement and exploration.

Persona

The project primarily focused on supporting family visitors who often experience crowded conditions while balancing education and play.

Challenge & Solution

How might we encourage visitors to explore underutilized areas of the Vancouver Aquarium while supporting different engagement styles during crowded moments?

We designed the Arctic Projection Tunnel, an immersive interactive experience that transforms an underused area into a destination.Through projections, audio, and motion-based interactions, the tunnel encourages self-directed exploration while supporting learning, engagement, and crowd redistribution.

Reflection

This project taught me the importance of reframing design problems rather than immediately seeking solutions. Initially, our team focused on reducing crowding within the aquarium. Through instructor feedback and further research, we realized that the stronger opportunity was to encourage exploration of overlooked spaces instead. This shift helped me better understand how observation, user needs, and iterative feedback can guide design decisions toward more meaningful experiences.
